Step 1

The garment

Shirts, T-shirts, trousers and jeans for now. The rest of the vocabulary exists, but each category needs its measuring instructions written properly, so they arrive when someone asks for them. Tell us which you want.

Use the size printed on its own, such as XL or 41. Many labels add a grid of conversions (I 46, F 42, D 40…): leave the grid, and choose the system of the size you used.

Leave it empty if you don't know it.

Copy the care label as it is written.

Pull the fabric gently across. If it gives and springs back, it stretches. Most T-shirts are “clearly”; a shirt or jeans with a few percent of elastane on the label is usually “a little”.

Step 2

Lay it flat

The garment is laid flat, fastened, and not stretched. Widths are taken perpendicular to the centre line. Leave out anything you cannot measure rather than estimating it: a gap is useful, a guess is not.

This is about the tape in your hand, not about the garment. A shirt labelled in one country can be measured with any tape: if yours is marked in centimetres, choose centimetres, whatever the size label says.

Step 4

Who measured it

This says where the numbers came from. It does not say they are right.

What the garment was meant to dobrand or manufacturer only

These four fields ask what the garment was designed to do, not what the tape says. Only whoever drew or produced it can answer them.

Choose the brand or the manufacturer above to fill these in. As the owner of a garment you can measure it exactly, but you cannot know what ease its designer intended, and an invented value is worse than an absent one.
